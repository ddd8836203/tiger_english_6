### 1. Topic 4 Intro: How We Talk (Student Book p.49)

There are many languages around the world. People who can't hear or speak use special languages called **sign languages**.

In sign languages, people "talk" using their hands to make signals. They also communicate by making different faces and moving their bodies. In American sign language, for instance, you can say "thank you" by putting your fingers on your chin and moving your hand out. This is also the sign for "you're welcome."

- **Questions**:
  1. How do you use language in school?
  2. How does learning a language help you?
  3. Would you like to learn a sign language? Why?

### 4. Understand (Student Book p.52 - p.53)

#### A. Think about the bar graph and text. Answer the questions. (p.52)
1. **What information does the bar graph show?**
   - *Answer*: `It shows the number of official languages in 196 countries around the world.`
2. **What does a country need to do if it wants to have an official language?**
   - *Answer*: `A country has to pass a law to make a language official.`
3. **How many countries have no official language?**
   - *Answer*: `8 countries.`
4. **How does the bar graph help you better understand the text?**
   - *Answer*: `It shows how common or rare it is for countries to have 0, 1, 2, 3, or more than 4 official languages.`

#### B. Choose the correct answer. (p.52)
1. Which is an official language of India?
   - [ ] a. Chinese
   - [x] **b. Hindi** *(and English)*
   - [ ] c. Spanish
   - [ ] d. none of the above
2. In the bar graph, which color bar would include Zimbabwe?
   - [ ] a. Orange (1)
   - [ ] b. Yellow (2)
   - [ ] c. Pink (3)
   - [x] **d. Purple (4+)** *(Zimbabwe has 16 official languages)*
3. In the bar graph, which country is included in the bar for 1?
   - [ ] a. China
   - [ ] b. Mexico
   - [ ] c. Zimbabwe
   - [x] **d. France** *(French is the single official language)*
4. Where might you *not* use an official language?
   - [ ] a. in government
   - [ ] b. while traveling
   - [x] **c. at home** *(people usually speak their native language at home)*
   - [ ] d. at school

#### D. Complete the sentences. (p.53)
*Word Bank*: `Chinese` | `English` | `French` | `Hindi` | `Mexican` | `native speakers` | `official languages` | `Spanish`

A long time ago, people from Spain brought their language to Mexico. Today, (1) **Mexican** people speak (2) **Spanish**. Similarly, language traveled from England to the United States, so many people in the United States speak (3) **English**.  
Most people in the United States and Mexico are (4) **native speakers** of either English or Spanish. People from all over the world have moved to these countries, too. People from China brought the (5) **Chinese** language with them. Many people from India brought (6) **Hindi**. People from France brought their native language, (7) **French**.  
And yet the United States and Mexico are countries with no (8) **official languages**! Neither country has passed a law to make one.

#### E. Reading Passage: My Languages (p.53)
```text
My Languages
My name is Tawanda. I grew up in a small village in Zimbabwe. The country has sixteen official languages. My native language is called Shona, and I also learned English in school. I know a few words in other languages, but don't speak or read them. When I was nineteen years old, I moved to Tanzania, a country near Zimbabwe. English and Swahili are the official languages here. So I'm learning Swahili now, too.
```
- **Comprehension Questions & Key**:
  1. Tawanda moved from **Zimbabwe** to **Tanzania** when he was nineteen.
  2. His family spoke a native language called **Shona** at home.
  3. Tawanda speaks **Shona / English** and is learning **Swahili**.
  4. Tanzania has two **official** languages.
